About the project

Sources, methods and limits of the Oak Forest research.

Oak Forest is one of the oldest villages in Greene County, Pennsylvania, settled long before the coal boom of the early twentieth century. Its land now lies above the Cumberland Mine, one of the largest underground coal mines in the state, and most of it belongs to the company that runs the mine. These pages ask a simple question about the village, whose land is this?, and follow it in three directions: through the deed records, through a century and a half of maps and aerial photographs, and down to the coal seam itself.

The research and maps are by Julian Giordano. Each page states its own sources; this page gathers them, with the methods behind each figure and what the evidence can and cannot show.

Property mapWho owns the village?

Open the property map →

The map shows who owns each of the 31 parcels in the village of Oak Forest today, when a coal company first bought it, and how the land changed hands. Coal companies own 26 of the 31 parcels, covering 255 of the village's 474 listed acres (54 percent). They first bought village land in three waves: 1998–2003, 2007–2011 and 2016–2021; nine parcels were first bought in 2017 alone. Two parcels were bought by a coal company and later sold back to private owners.

For the village, the deeds are the authoritative record. Where they differ from the county's online sales history, the deeds stand. The county records supply only the tax ID, the listed acreage and the owner's classification.

What counts as a coal company

An owner counts as a coal company if its principal business is producing coal, or if it is a land-holding affiliate of such a company, or if it holds land that was bought for or used in coal mining. Each owner name receives one label:

LabelRuleCounted as coal?
CoalCoal mining or preparation; a subsidiary, land company or affiliate of a coal producer; or land bought for or used in coal miningYes
GasOil and gas production, gathering, pipelines or storageNo
UtilityElectric generation or transmissionNo
Not coalIndividuals, government bodies, institutions and all other businessesNo
  • Surface and minerals are separate estates. In Greene County the coal was usually sold apart from the land above it, often generations ago. The map concerns the surface.
  • Contractors, utilities and cleanup bodies are not coal companies, even when their work is tied to mining.
  • Past owners are labelled by the same rules, but only for the land they held for coal mining. A company that is coal for one holding is not thereby coal for all of its holdings.

On the map, coal owners are grouped by company (for example, the land companies in the chain of ownership of the Cumberland Mine), with the name as recorded shown beside the group.

How the deed histories were recorded

  • Recording. Each parcel's deeds are listed newest to oldest by book and page, date, grantee (the owner after the conveyance) and consideration. Where one deed conveyed several parcels, the entry applies to each of them; a parcel assembled from several tracts carries a separate chain for each tract.
  • Prices. Where a deed or the county shows $0 or a nominal sum, the consideration was calculated from the realty transfer tax recorded on the deed (state and local tax are each 1 percent of the price). Nominal sums that remain ($1, $10) reflect mergers, conveyances within a family, or "other good and valuable consideration," and are marked "nominal." A deed covering land in several townships states one price for all of it.
  • Depth. Every chain is partial. Nearly all could be traced further back, and a chain is treated as complete only where it reaches the founding of the village; none yet does. Dates are given as recorded; where execution and recording differ, both are noted.
  • Notes on deeds (on tax sales, wills and estates, corporate mergers and name changes, and the rights reserved to coal companies) are shown with the deed they concern. In one 1999 sale back to a family, the coal company kept the coal, oil and gas, the right to let the surface subside, a pipeline easement for coal-bed methane, and a right of entry until reclamation was complete.
  • No street addresses are used: several are shared by more than one parcel, and some parcels have none.

Limits

  • Chains of title are partial, as above.
  • Assessment records are not legal title. They show the owner of record for taxation; deeds, leases and unrecorded transfers can differ.
  • Parcels outside the village show county assessment data only; their deeds have not been researched.
  • Boundaries are approximate. The state parcel layer does not always follow current parcel lines.
  • Ownership is a snapshot as of November 2026; coal land in Greene County changes hands often.

3D modelPanel 75, beneath the village

Open Panel 75 in 3D →

Longwall panel 75 of the Cumberland Mine is a block of the Pittsburgh coal seam 11,221 ft long and 1,393 ft wide, mined from July 2022 to January 2023, 600 to 800 ft below the surface. It passes under the village. The model shows the land above it and the panel at its true depth, seen through the ground.

The land

  • Ground: USGS 3DEP lidar elevation. Streams traced from the lidar. Trees from the PEMA 2022 orthophoto, with estimated heights. Buildings: in Oak Forest traced from the 2022 orthophoto; elsewhere Microsoft building footprints. Roads and power lines: orthophoto and OpenStreetMap.
  • Heights are true unless "3×" is chosen. Trees and buildings are never exaggerated.

The mine

  • Panels: PA DEP longwall mining panels. Seam depth: PA DEP coal seam elevations (from Cumberland Mine maps), fitted with a smooth surface for each panel.
  • The panel is drawn ten times its 7 ft height so that it can be seen at the scale of the land (the seam height is an estimate). In the close-up shearer view it is drawn at its true height.
  • Mining over time: month-end positions of the longwall face from the Cumberland Mine subsidence map (96th projection, sheet 31-4). Between month ends the face is drawn moving at an even pace; where a panel's first or last stretch has no date, its timing is estimated.
  • Gate roads: three entries on each side of the panel, with their pillars and angled crosscuts, measured on sheet 31-4 and continued at the same spacing beyond the sheet. They are a sketch, not a survey.
  • Neighbouring panels: Cumberland panels 73 and 74 and Emerald Mine panels 21 Right and 6 Right (1993), each placed on its own fit to the seam elevations. Parts of the Emerald panels lie beneath the block and are always shown; the rest can be switched on.

The longwall

  • The shearer cuts a 42-inch web on each pass across the 1,393 ft face, in both directions (Cumberland's practice as reported in American Longwall Magazine, May 2005), so panel 75 took about 3,200 passes. The same report names the mine's shearer in 2005 as a Joy 7LS2, a double-drum ranging shearer; the model is a generic machine of that kind, not a drawing of the one that mined panel 75. Its position along the face is worked out from the face advance, not recorded.
  • The shields (hydraulic roof supports) are drawn as two-leg shields 1.75 m (5.7 ft) wide, the standard width for the Pittsburgh seam, so about 240 of them line the face. Behind the shearer each shield in turn lowers, pulls itself forward one web and resets against the roof, and the face conveyor is then pushed forward in a gentle S-bend. Behind the shields the roof caves into the mined-out space (the gob).
  • A real pass at about 40 ft a minute takes roughly 35 minutes. The model plays a pass in 30 seconds by default (about 70 times real speed), with slower settings.

Subsidence (modelled, not measured)

Over a longwall panel the ground sinks as the coal is removed. The model shows a subsidence trough calculated from general figures for Pittsburgh-seam longwalls in Greene County, not from survey data for panel 75:

  • Depth of the trough: about 4 ft at the centre of the panel, taking subsidence as about 55 percent of the 7 ft mining height (DEP-compiled figures for nearby Pittsburgh-seam longwalls give 53–60 percent).
  • Extent: the trough reaches beyond the panel edges at an angle of draw of about 15° from the vertical (roughly 200 ft at this depth), with the classic smooth S-shaped edge.
  • Timing: a small part of the movement happens as the face passes beneath; the rest develops over the following months, after a U.S. Bureau of Mines study in Greene County in which subsidence at the panel centre continued for about seven months after undermining.
  • Drawn 10× deeper than calculated, so that a sag of a few feet can be seen at the scale of a two-mile block. The cracks drawn in the rock above the mined panel mark the broken ground over a longwall (the caved and fractured zones) in a general way; their positions are illustrative.
  • The ground surface is the lidar as it was surveyed; any subsidence already in it is not removed.

Subsidence sources: PA DEP, "Effects of Longwall Mining on Real Property Value and the Tax Base of Greene and Washington Counties" (Resource Technologies Corp., 2002); PA DEP longwall forestland study, table of longwall mine data; University of Pittsburgh, Act 54 assessments for PA DEP.

Names and correctionsNames, privacy and corrections

Owners' names come from public deed and tax records. No street addresses are shown, and the 3D model names no one. People named on the property map, or their families, can ask for a correction or for a name to be withheld.
